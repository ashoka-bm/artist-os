# Kishotenketsu

**Entry id**: `kishotenketsu`

**Scope**: A four-part movement introduces a world, develops it, introduces an unexpected turn, and reconciles the turn with what came before.

**Structure grounding**: Kishotenketsu four-part narrative tradition.

**Grounding status**: `researched`

**Grounding tier**: `canonical_framework`

**Use when**: The work needs discovery, juxtaposition, contrast, or recontextualization without requiring conflict as the central engine.

**Core movement**: The work presents a situation, develops its pattern, introduces a turn or apparently separate element, then lets the audience understand the first parts differently through reconciliation.

**Typical Beat Roles**:

| Role | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Ki / introduction | Presents the situation, world, subject, or first image. |
| Sho / development | Deepens the first material without forcing escalation. |
| Ten / turn | Introduces surprise, contrast, complication, or a new angle. |
| Ketsu / reconciliation | Connects the turn back to the opening material. |

**Key turns**:

- Development: the audience learns the pattern well enough to feel a later shift.
- Turn: new material changes the frame.
- Reconciliation: the work creates coherence without needing victory or defeat.

**Compression guidance**: In compact works, give each part one clear move. The turn can be one image, sentence, sound change, cut, or argument shift.

**Expansion guidance**: In long works, repeat the four-part movement at section or chapter scale. Keep the turn meaningful, not random, and let reconciliation carry emotional logic.

**Medium affinities**: Short stories, comics, poems, reflective essays, image sequences, video essays, quiet films, sound pieces, and works where contrast matters more than confrontation.

**Common failure modes**:

- The turn is arbitrary and does not reframe the first parts.
- The structure is mistaken for "nothing happens."
- Conflict is banned even when Artist Meaning needs it.
- Reconciliation explains the turn instead of letting the work click.
- The entry is treated as an exotic alternative rather than a usable movement pattern.

**Adaptation questions**:

- What pattern does the audience need to learn first?
- What turn changes the frame without breaking the work?
- How does the ending reconcile the turn with the opening?
- Does the work need conflict, or only contrast?
- What should feel newly visible at the end?
