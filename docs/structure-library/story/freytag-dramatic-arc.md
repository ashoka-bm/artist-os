# Freytag Dramatic Arc

**Entry id**: `freytag_dramatic_arc`

**Scope**: A dramatic work rises through pressure to a central turning point, then falls through consequence into resolution, catastrophe, or denouement.

**Structure grounding**: Freytag dramatic arc.

**Grounding status**: `researched`

**Grounding tier**: `canonical_framework`

**Use when**: The work needs visible escalation, a peak turn, and an aftermath that matters as much as the climb.

**Core movement**: The work establishes a situation, introduces an exciting force, rises toward the strongest point of pressure, turns at the climax, then follows the consequences into a final state.

**Typical Beat Roles**:

| Role | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Exposition | Establishes setting, relationship, pressure, and what stability looks like. |
| Exciting force | Starts the action that pushes the work upward. |
| Rising action | Builds pressure through increasingly consequential movement. |
| Climax | Changes the governing condition of the work. |
| Falling action | Shows the cost, reversal, release, or collapse after the peak. |
| Final suspense | Briefly keeps the final outcome alive before closure. |
| Catastrophe / denouement | Lands the final result and emotional residue. |

**Key turns**:

- Exciting force: the dramatic line begins.
- Climax: the work reaches its strongest pressure and changes direction.
- Falling action: consequence replaces escalation.
- Final suspense: the work tests whether another outcome remains possible.
- Denouement: the dramatic pressure resolves or leaves its final wound.

**Compression guidance**: In short works, merge exposition with exciting force, let rising action carry one strong escalation, and treat the ending as consequence rather than explanation.

**Expansion guidance**: In long works, track the rise and fall separately. Do not spend every sequence climbing. Let the falling side reveal cost, recognition, reversal, and emotional accounting.

**Medium affinities**: Drama, tragedy, short fiction, theatre, film, narrative poetry, essays with a crisis-and-consequence shape, and visual or sound sequences built around a peak turn.

**Common failure modes**:

- The climax arrives but nothing changes.
- Falling action becomes summary instead of consequence.
- The rise uses volume rather than pressure.
- The ending ties off plot but not emotional residue.
- A comic, reflective, or open-ended work is forced into tragic descent.

**Adaptation questions**:

- What starts the dramatic rise?
- What pressure keeps increasing?
- What exactly changes at the climax?
- What consequence must the audience sit with after the peak?
- Does the final state resolve, wound, expose, or release the work?
